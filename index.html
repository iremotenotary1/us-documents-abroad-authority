U.S. documents abroad: notarization, consular options, and authentication

This iRemoteNotary educational resource collects selected guidance and public sources about U.S. documents signed or used while a person is outside the United States. It covers remote online notarization, consular and notarial alternatives, apostille and authentication, and the practical need to confirm what the receiving party will accept.

01

U.S. documents while abroad

People outside the United States are often asked to complete U.S.-related affidavits, powers of attorney, passport-consent forms, company records, estate papers, proof-of-life or life-certificate forms, and similar documents. The required notarial act, witnesses, and return method depend on the document and on the party that will receive it.

02

Notarization is not apostille or authentication

A notarization records a notarial act on a signature, oath, or similar request. An apostille or authentication is a separate government certification used in some cross-border filings. Completing a remote online notarization does not mean the document qualifies for apostille, authentication, or use before a foreign authority.

03

Recipient acceptance controls the path

An attorney, title company, lender, court, agency, financial institution, or foreign authority may require a specific format, an in-person appearance, an original wet-ink paper, a consular act, or additional legalization. Confirm those instructions before booking. iRemoteNotary does not determine legal validity and does not guarantee that a completed session will be accepted, recorded, or legalized.

04

International signer preparation

On supported platforms, iRemoteNotary’s international workflow is biometrics-first. Signers should have a supported government-issued identity document available for credential analysis and biometric identity verification. Knowledge-based authentication is not the normal international workflow. Not every credential, device, or location is accepted on every platform.
